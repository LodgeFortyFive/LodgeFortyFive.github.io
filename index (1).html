About

Brotherhood.
Education. Service.

Lodge No. 45 is a lodge of Free and Accepted Masons in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Our stated meetings are held at the Greater Pittsburgh Masonic Center on the last Wednesday of each month, with degree work, education, fellowship and service throughout the year.

The Records

History is not behind us.

It is beneath our feet: layer upon layer of names, warrants, meetings, losses, mergers and service. Descend into the record of No. 45 and the darkness gives way—not away from the past, but through it.

1780s
Before No. 45

Fort Pitt and Lodge No. 19.

According to the Lodge history, the Provincial Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania granted Colonel Thomas Proctor of the Pennsylvania Artillery, stationed at Fort Pitt, a charter for a military lodge. Lodge No. 19 continued until December 20, 1784. Its last known warrant holder was Captain Isaac Craig—who would become a charter member of No. 45.

1785
Institution

December 27, 1785.

On that date Lodge No. 45 was chartered by the Provincial Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania, acting by virtue of a warrant from the Grand Lodge of England. The record links the Lodge directly to the Masonic presence at Fort Pitt through Isaac Craig.

1786
A Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania

Present at the beginning.

The Lodge history records that, after the organization in 1786 of the Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania, No. 45 assisted in its formation and received a charter from that body. From its organization onward, the history describes the career of No. 45 as uninterrupted.

1798
An object survives

The ancient seal.

An ancient seal of Lodge No. 45 appears on the certificate of Brother William Thorn dated November 6, 1798, preserved in the archives of the Grand Lodge. More than two centuries later, it remains a physical mark from the Lodge's earliest years.

West of the Alleghenies
“Lodge No. 45 is the Mother of the lodges, being the parent Lodge of the city.”
From the Lodge historical foreword
1785—
Continuity

From wilderness to city.

The Lodge's own historical account places its beginning when western Pennsylvania was still a wilderness and describes more than two centuries of labor and influence throughout the West and Southwest. Its charge to later brethren is not merely to remember that inheritance, but to preserve and transmit it to future generations.
