About

Brotherhood.
Education. Service.

Lodge No. 45 is a local chapter of Freemasonry in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Freemasonry is a centuries-old fraternal tradition built around friendship, moral self-improvement, charity, ceremony and service. Members meet regularly, learn the traditions of the fraternity, support one another and serve their communities.

New here? Start with the language.

Tap a question for a quick explanation. The old language stays; the mystery does not have to.

What is a Mason?

A Freemason is a member of the fraternity. Members commonly call one another “Brother.”

What is a Lodge?

The local group of Masons—and, depending on context, the place where that group meets. No. 45 is one such local lodge.

What is a Grand Lodge?

The governing Masonic organization for a state or jurisdiction. Lodge No. 45 operates under the Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania.

What is a Warrant?

The formal authorization that allows a lodge to exist and work as a recognized Masonic lodge—essentially its founding charter.

What are Degrees?

Freemasonry teaches through three ceremonial stages: Entered Apprentice, Fellow Craft and Master Mason. “Degree work” means presenting those ceremonies.

Why the old titles?

Titles such as Worshipful Master and Warden are centuries-old offices preserved by the fraternity. They sound antique because they are.

The Records

History is not behind us.

For more than two centuries, the story of No. 45 has been tied to the story of Pittsburgh itself—soldiers, builders, physicians, civic institutions, disaster relief and generations of men expected to serve something larger than themselves.

1780s
Before No. 45

Fort Pitt and Lodge No. 19.

According to the Lodge history, Pennsylvania’s Masonic governing body—then called the Provincial Grand Lodge—authorized Colonel Thomas Proctor of the Pennsylvania Artillery, stationed at Fort Pitt, to operate a military lodge. Lodge No. 19 continued until December 20, 1784. Its last known warrant holder was Captain Isaac Craig—who would become a charter member of No. 45.

1785
Institution

December 27, 1785.

On that date Lodge No. 45 received its warrant—its formal authorization to exist as a Masonic lodge—from Pennsylvania’s Masonic governing body, which at the time still traced its authority to the Grand Lodge of England. The record links the Lodge directly to the Masonic presence at Fort Pitt through Isaac Craig.

1786
A Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania

Present at the beginning.

The Lodge history records that, after Pennsylvania Freemasons declared their organization independent of British authority in 1786, No. 45 assisted in forming the new Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania—the statewide governing organization for Pennsylvania lodges—and thereafter operated under its authority. The Lodge survived later interruptions in meetings without being dissolved or reorganized.

1798
An object survives

The ancient seal.

An ancient seal of Lodge No. 45 appears on the certificate of William Thorn dated November 6, 1798, preserved in the archives of the Grand Lodge. More than two centuries later, it remains a physical mark from the Lodge's earliest years.

1809–11
A permanent home

No. 45 builds on Wood Street.

In 1809, No. 45 undertook an ambitious project: a lodge building of its own. Land was secured on Wood Street near Fifth Street—today's Fifth Avenue—and held in trust for the Lodge. The building was completed by June 24, 1811, when No. 45 invited Lodge No. 113 to join a procession marking its dedication. In a young Pittsburgh still taking shape around it, the Lodge now had a permanent home downtown.

1815
Sharing the hall

Another lodge moves in.

When Lodge No. 113 became dissatisfied with its own meeting room, No. 45 agreed to accommodate it. The two lodges shared the Wood Street building; surviving records show No. 113 paying rent first at $48 and later $110 per year.

1822
A changing city

The Wood Street building is sold.

No. 45 sold its building to Benjamin Darlington for $1,250 and moved into rented quarters. The former lodge building became associated with Darlington's Hotel, also known as the Mansion House, at Wood and Fifth.

1825
A Pittsburgh story

Lafayette and the old lodge room.

A later nineteenth-century recollection connected the former lodge room with the Marquis de Lafayette's 1825 visit to Pittsburgh. The room was remembered for an arched ceiling painted with the Sun, Moon and Stars, and the account says Lafayette slept there while staying at the Mansion House. The story comes from a later recollection rather than the Lodge's surviving minutes.

1829–31
Third & Smithfield

A shared Masonic Hall downtown.

Pittsburgh's lodges had spent years discussing a common hall. In 1829 No. 45 invested $500 in the McGill & Darsie project, then added another $500 in 1830. The resulting Masonic Hall stood at Third and Smithfield Streets. No. 45 was using it by the end of 1830; the name “Masonic Hall” appears in its surviving minutes in March 1831.

1845
The Great Fire · Relief & Charity

The Great Fire reaches the Lodge.

The Great Fire of Pittsburgh swept through downtown on April 10 and destroyed the Masonic Hall and its contents. Five days later, No. 45 met again at a member's office. The Independent Order of Odd Fellows offered the Lodge free use of its hall for six months, while No. 45 appointed a committee to identify and assist members who had suffered losses. Appeals for relief brought charitable contributions from fellow Masons and lodges in Savannah, Cincinnati, Kentucky, Wheeling, St. Clairsville, Steubenville and Philadelphia. By October 29, No. 45 was meeting in a new “Masonic Temple” at Wood and Third Streets.

A Lodge in the Life of a City

Not just meetings. Men expected to serve.

The old records are full of titles and lodge business. The more important story is what these men did outside the lodge room. The founders and early members of No. 45 helped defend the frontier, build institutions, organize public works and respond when Pittsburgh was in trouble.

Revolutionary Generation

Soldiers became city builders.

Charter member Isaac Craig served in the Revolutionary War, was wounded at Brandywine and later came to Fort Pitt. In civilian life he helped develop early Pittsburgh, worked on Fort Fayette, helped establish an Ohio River mail service and later joined James O’Hara in an early glassworks west of the Alleghenies.

Service after service
1786–1787

Helping build Pittsburgh’s institutions.

Charter members Robert Galbraith and William Butler, and later Lodge Master and physician Nathaniel Bedford, were connected with the Pittsburgh Academy—the institution that became the University of Pittsburgh. Bedford also served the growing borough as a civic leader.

Education & civic life
1845 · The Great Fire of Pittsburgh

When the city burned, the response was people helping people.

The fire destroyed the Masonic Hall, but the surviving Lodge record is more revealing than the lost building. No. 45 appointed men to determine which members had suffered in the disaster and how badly, while fellow fraternal organizations and Masons in cities across the country sent help to Pittsburgh. The Independent Order of Odd Fellows gave the displaced lodge meeting space free for six months. It is an early snapshot of the mutual-aid network these organizations created long before modern disaster-relief systems.

Relief · Mutual aid · Pittsburgh
West of the Alleghenies
“Lodge No. 45 is the Mother of the lodges, being the parent Lodge of the city.”
From the Lodge historical foreword

That old description points in one direction: outward. As Pittsburgh grew, Freemasonry grew with it. New lodges formed, each building a history, membership and identity of its own.

The Family Returns

Separate lodges. Shared inheritance.

By the late twentieth century, the story began to move in the other direction. Several Pittsburgh-area lodges joined with No. 45. They did not arrive without a past: each brought decades of meetings, service, traditions and men who had led their lodge. Their individual charters ended, but their histories did not. Those histories are preserved here as part of the larger story now carried by Lodge No. 45.
